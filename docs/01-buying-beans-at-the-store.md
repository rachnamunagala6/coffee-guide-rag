# Buying Coffee Beans at the Store

## Check the roast date first
The single most useful thing on a bag of coffee is the roast date. Coffee is at its best roughly 4 days to 4 weeks after roasting. In the first few days it is still releasing carbon dioxide, which can make brewing uneven; after about a month it steadily loses aroma and tastes flat.

Look for a printed "roasted on" date. A "best by" or "use by" date is far less useful because it is often set 6 to 12 months after roasting and tells you nothing about freshness. If a bag has only a best-by date, assume it was roasted long ago. At a grocery store, dig toward the back of the shelf for the most recent roast date.

## Whole bean versus pre-ground
Buy whole beans whenever you can. Ground coffee exposes far more surface area to air and loses much of its aroma within minutes to hours of grinding. Whole beans stay fresh for weeks. If you do not own a grinder, many specialty shops and some grocery stores will grind beans for you; tell them your brew method (for example "French press" or "drip machine") so they use the right grind size, and buy a smaller bag so you finish it quickly.

## Look at the packaging
- A one-way degassing valve (a small round plastic button on the bag) lets carbon dioxide out without letting oxygen in. It is a sign the roaster cares about freshness.
- Resealable bags with a zip or tin tie are convenient for storage.
- Avoid clear bags and open bulk bins. Light, air and other shoppers' scoops all speed up staling, and bulk bins rarely show a roast date.
- Nitrogen-flushed bags and cans keep coffee fresh longer, which makes them a reasonable choice when no roast date is available.

## Arabica versus Robusta
Most quality coffee is 100% Arabica, which has more sweetness, acidity and aromatic complexity. Robusta has roughly twice the caffeine, a heavier and more bitter, rubbery or chocolatey flavor, and good crema in espresso. Many Italian-style espresso blends deliberately include some Robusta. "100% Arabica" on a label is common and does not by itself mean the coffee is high quality.

## Reading the label: origin, process and tasting notes
- **Single origin** coffee comes from one country, region or farm and shows that place's character. **Blends** combine origins for balance and consistency and are often designed for espresso.
- **Region and farm details** (for example "Yirgacheffe, Ethiopia" rather than just "Ethiopia") usually signal more traceable, higher-quality coffee.
- **Altitude** is sometimes listed. Higher-grown coffee (above about 1,200 m or 4,000 ft) tends to be denser, brighter and more complex.
- **Processing method** has a big effect on flavor. See the flavor guide for washed, natural and honey processing.
- **Tasting notes** such as "blueberry, cocoa" describe flavors the coffee naturally suggests. Nothing is added. They are the best quick hint of whether you will like a coffee.
- **Roast level** is described in the flavor guide.

## Specialty roaster versus supermarket
Local or specialty roasters usually print roast dates, roast in small batches and sell coffee within weeks of roasting. Supermarket coffee is cheaper and convenient but often older. Many grocery stores now stock local roasters; those bags are usually the best choice on the shelf. Staff at a specialty shop can recommend a coffee if you describe what you like and how you brew.

## Decaf
Decaffeinated coffee can taste very good. Common methods are the Swiss Water Process (water and carbon filters, no added chemicals), CO2 processing, and the "sugarcane" or ethyl acetate method (EA). Methylene chloride is also used and is considered safe at the trace levels left after roasting, but people who prefer to avoid solvents often choose Swiss Water or CO2. Labels usually state the method.

## Certifications
Fair Trade, Rainforest Alliance and USDA Organic labels address farmer pay, environmental practices or farming inputs. They say little about flavor or freshness. Some specialty roasters pay well above these standards through direct relationships with farms and describe that on the bag or their website.

## How much to buy
Buy only what you will use within 2 to 3 weeks. For one person drinking two cups a day, that is about a 12 oz (340 g) bag every 2 weeks. A typical cup uses 15 to 20 g of beans.

## Price as a quality signal
Very cheap coffee is almost always old, low-grade or heavily dark-roasted to hide defects. Specialty coffee typically costs about $14 to $25 for 12 oz in the US, with rare coffees costing more. Paying more beyond that does not always mean you will enjoy it more; freshness and matching your taste matter more than price.

## Quick store checklist
1. Roast date within the last 2 to 3 weeks.
2. Whole bean, in an opaque bag with a valve.
3. Roast level and tasting notes that match your taste.
4. A size you will finish within a few weeks.
5. If buying ground, the grind matched to your brewer.
