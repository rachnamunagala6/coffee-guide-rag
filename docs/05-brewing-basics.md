# Brewing Basics: Ratio, Water and Temperature

## The coffee-to-water ratio
The ratio of coffee to water is the main control over how strong your coffee tastes.
- A good starting point for most hot brewing (drip, pour-over, French press) is **1:16**: 1 gram of coffee for every 16 grams of water. That is about 15 g coffee for 250 g (about 8.5 oz) of water, or 60 g of coffee per liter.
- For stronger coffee use 1:14 or 1:15. For lighter coffee use 1:17 or 1:18.
- Espresso is much more concentrated, around 1:2 (coffee weight to liquid espresso weight).
- Cold brew concentrate is usually 1:5 to 1:8, then diluted.

## Measuring without a scale
A level tablespoon of whole beans weighs roughly 5 g, and a level tablespoon of ground coffee about 5 to 6 g, though it varies with roast and grind. A common rule of thumb is 2 level tablespoons of ground coffee per 6 fl oz (180 ml) of water. A kitchen scale that reads in grams is inexpensive and makes your coffee far more consistent.

## Water
Coffee is about 98% water, so water quality matters.
- Use filtered water if your tap water tastes of chlorine or is very hard.
- Avoid distilled or reverse-osmosis water on its own. It has no minerals, which makes coffee taste flat and can damage some machines.
- Always use fresh, cold water to start.

## Water temperature
Brew between **195°F and 205°F (90°C to 96°C)**. Without a thermometer, bring water to a boil and let it rest for about 30 seconds. Water that is too hot can extract harsh, bitter flavors; water that is too cool leaves coffee sour and weak. Lighter roasts benefit from the hotter end of the range, darker roasts from the cooler end.

## The bloom
Freshly roasted coffee releases carbon dioxide when hot water hits it. Pouring a small amount of water first (about twice the weight of the coffee) and waiting 30 to 45 seconds lets the gas escape so the rest of the water extracts evenly. This is called the bloom and is used in pour-over and often in French press. A strong bloom is a sign of fresh coffee.

## Extraction in one paragraph
Brewing dissolves flavor compounds from the grounds. Acids come out first, then sugars, then bitter compounds. **Under-extracted** coffee (too little contact or too coarse) tastes sour, salty and thin. **Over-extracted** coffee (too much contact, too fine or too hot) tastes bitter, harsh and drying. Good coffee lands in between. Grind size, brew time, water temperature and agitation are the levers.

## Equipment that makes the biggest difference
In order of impact: fresh beans, a burr grinder, a kitchen scale, then a kettle (a gooseneck kettle helps with pour-over). Expensive brewers matter less than these.

## Keep equipment clean
Coffee oils turn rancid and make coffee taste bitter. Rinse brewers after each use, and descale coffee machines and kettles every 1 to 3 months depending on water hardness, following the maker's instructions.
